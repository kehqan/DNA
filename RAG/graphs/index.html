How the assistant finds the right rule

There are circles and lines in the technical documents for this project. This page explains what they mean, using something every newsroom already understands — the investigation board with pins and string.

If you have ever pinned photographs to a wall and run red string between them, you have already built the thing this page is about.

Engineers call it a knowledge graph. It is a diagram of things and the connections between them. The circles are the things. The lines are the connections. That is genuinely all there is to the shape of it.

What follows is why the assistant needs one — and it starts with a failure that has nothing to do with computers.

01

Start with the failure

Imagine you hand a new intern the newsroom standards manual and one sentence from a draft. You ask: which rules apply here?

The intern has never read the manual. So they do the obvious thing — they flip through looking for pages that use the same words as the sentence. That is exactly how ordinary computer search works, and it works most of the time.

Try it — this is a real failure, not a strawman
A senior official, who asked not to be named, told us the ministry approved the deal.

Read the second rule in the "actually apply" list carefully. It is the one that says a second source is not required when finding one would put the first source in danger. It contains none of the words in the sentence. Not "official". Not "named". Not "ministry".

Word-matching cannot find it. Not because the search is badly built — because the connection between that sentence and that rule is not made of words. It is made of a relationship: one rule modifies another. And relationships are exactly what a board with string is for.

Click a pin above. The panel will explain what that rule says and what it is connected to.
The one thing worth remembering

The orange dashed string is the important one. It means "this rule changes how that rule works" — an exception, a carve-out, a condition. Those are the connections that a search-by-words approach can never see, and the reason the board exists at all.

Nobody drew this board by hand. Each rule is written down by an editor in a simple file, and in that file the editor writes something like "see also R-410" or "this rule is modified by R-410." The board is then drawn automatically from what the editors wrote. If an editor removes a connection, the string disappears the next day. The board can never say something the editors did not say.

03

Two different ways to be relevant

Once you have a board, there are two completely different ways a rule can turn out to matter for a sentence you have written.

Way one — it sounds like the sentence

The rule uses similar words. Your sentence says "asked not to be named", the rule says "anonymous sources" — close enough that a computer can spot the resemblance.

Good at: finding the obvious rule quickly.
Blind to: anything phrased differently from how you wrote it.

Way two — it sits in the right part of the board

The rule has a lot of string running to it, and that string leads back toward the rules your sentence obviously touches. It is near the topic even when it does not sound like it.

Good at: finding the serious rule you did not phrase in its language.
Blind to: nothing much — but on its own it is vague.

Neither is right on its own. Way one alone misses the danger exception. Way two alone would keep suggesting the same few heavily-connected rules for everything, like a colleague who answers every question with "well, have you considered defamation?"

So the assistant uses both, and mixes them. That mixing is controlled by a single dial.

04

The dial

Drag it. The list underneath reorders as you do.

The same sentence, ranked differently as you turn the dial

Sounds like it Connected to it

Notice there is no single correct setting. Turn it all the way to "sounds like it" and you get a tidy, obvious list that quietly omits the rule that matters most. Turn it all the way to "connected to it" and the serious rules crowd in whether or not they are relevant today.

Where the setting comes from — this part matters

The dial is not set once for everything. Each family of rules gets its own setting, chosen deliberately:

Rule familyDial leans towardBecause
Legal & safetyConnectionsMissing one of these is serious. Better to surface it even when your wording gave no clue.
SourcingSlightly toward connectionsSourcing rules come with exceptions attached, and exceptions are found through string.
Style & terminologyWordingYou want the rule about that specific word. Missing a style note costs little.

These settings are editorial decisions, not technical ones. They say how cautious the assistant should be in each area — and they are written down where editors can see and change them, not buried in software.

05

Following the string

There is one more move, and it is the one that makes the whole system worth building. After the assistant has ranked the rules, it does something a ranking cannot do on its own: it follows the string.

Step through what actually happens

Stage three is the point. Rule R-410 was never going to win a ranking contest against your sentence — it shares no words with it and it is not an especially famous rule. It arrives for one reason: a piece of string connects it to a rule that did rank, and the assistant always follows the string.

Why this matters on a deadline

Without this step, the assistant would tell you "you need a second source" and stop. You would then have to remember, unprompted, that a safety exception exists and where to find it. With it, the exception arrives attached to the rule it modifies — so what you see is not "you broke a rule" but "here is the rule, and here is the door out of it, and here is who has to sign."

06

Why the circles are different sizes

Two things are encoded in how a pin looks, and both are worth being able to read at a glance.

  • Size is seriousness. Big pins are rules that stop publication until someone signs off. Medium pins need acknowledging. Small pins are advice you may take or leave. This is set by editors when they write the rule — never by the software.
  • Colour is family. Sourcing, legal, ethics, style, safety. Rules of the same family cluster in the same region of the board, which is why the board looks like clumps rather than a mesh.

There is also a quieter signal: how much string runs to a pin. A rule that many other rules point at is usually foundational — the kind of rule that has grown exceptions and clarifications over years. The assistant treats heavily-connected rules as more likely to be relevant, which is the "connections" half of the dial doing its work.

A fair question

Does that mean an old, much-amended rule outranks a newer, cleaner one? Sometimes, yes — and that is a real limitation worth naming rather than hiding. It is one of the things the testing phase is designed to measure, and one of the reasons every suggestion shows why it appeared rather than presenting itself as simply correct.

07

What this looks like when you are writing

None of the machinery above appears while you work. What appears is a note in the margin.

Roughly what a flag looks like
Needs a second source · stops publication
R-042 — Anonymous source corroboration. Claims from an unnamed source need two independent confirmations before publication.
Related — R-410: if seeking a second source would endanger the first, a senior editor may waive this. Their sign-off is logged.
Standards Manual v3, page 14 · reviewed March 2026 · owner: Managing Editor

Four things are deliberately true about that note, and each one is a promise the system is built to keep.

  • It names the rule. Not "this seems problematic" — a specific rule with a number you can look up.
  • It cites where the rule came from. Document and page. If you think the assistant has misread the rule, you can check in ten seconds.
  • It brings the exception with it. You are not left to remember that a waiver exists.
  • It names an owner. If the rule itself is wrong or out of date, there is a person to take that to.

And what it will not do

  • It does not approve your story. No flags means no rule was matched — it does not mean the piece is sound. It has no opinion on whether your reporting is any good.
  • It does not overrule you. Everything can be overridden. Overrides are logged with your reason, which is a record, not a reprimand — and those records are how the rulebook gets better.
  • It does not check facts. It compares your draft against the newsroom's own rules. It knows nothing about whether the ministry actually approved the deal.
  • It does not invent rules. If a rule is not written down, reviewed and owned, the assistant cannot cite it. It can only apply the rulebook as editors have written it.

The jargon, translated

If you sit in a meeting about this project, these are the words you will hear. Left column is what they say; right column is what they mean.

Knowledge graphalso: "the graph"
The board with pins and string. Things, and the connections between them.
Nodealso: entity, vertex
A pin. One rule, or one category, or one desk.
Edgealso: relationship, link
A piece of string. A connection between two pins.
Retrieval
Finding the rules that might apply to a sentence. Everything on this page is about retrieval.
Semantic similarity
"It sounds like it." How closely the wording of a rule resembles the wording of your sentence.
Graph relevancealso: PageRank
"It is connected to it." How central a rule is on the board, relative to what you wrote.
Lambda · λ
The dial. How much to weight sounding-like-it against being-connected-to-it. A number between 0 and 1.
Subgraph expansion
Following the string. Pulling in whatever is connected to a rule that was found, even if it would not have been found on its own.
Taxonomy
The fixed list of rule families — sourcing, legal, ethics, style, safety. Frozen on purpose so it cannot drift.
Severity
Pin size. Blocking, warning, or advisory.
Mechanical rule
A rule a computer can check with certainty — a banned word, a missing dateline. No judgement, no AI involved.
Judgement rule
A rule needing interpretation — tone, balance, whether sourcing is adequate. This is where the AI is used, and only here.
Shadow mode
Running the assistant silently on real drafts, recording what it would have said without showing anyone, to find out how accurate it is before it can interrupt anybody.
Precision & recall
Precision: of the flags it raised, how many were fair. Recall: of the problems that existed, how many it caught. Improving one usually worsens the other.

If you take one thing from this page

The board exists so that a rule can be found by its relationships, not only by its words — and the single most valuable thing it finds that way is the exception that lets you publish.

A system that only knew how to say no would be easy to build and impossible to work with. Following the string is what lets it say "yes, with this condition, signed off by this person" instead.
